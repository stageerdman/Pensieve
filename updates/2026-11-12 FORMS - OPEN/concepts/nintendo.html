Forms · UX concept round · Nintendo / Miyamoto team

A nightly ritual you want to come back to.

Thesis. A form is a little game with very few verbs. Mario has run and jump; a Pensieve form has Rate, Tick, Write. Learn each verb by doing it once, never by reading a manual. Every press answers back with a small, physical response: a stone that hops, a box that pops, a lantern that lights. Those responses are the reward. There are no points, badges or streak counters.

The evening gets pacing too (kishōtenketsu: arrive, build, turn, land). The easy ticks come first to build momentum, the honest questions come in the middle, and it ends on what to reward. Patronus helps the way a co-op player or a Mario Kart ghost does: it rides alongside in its own visibly different ink, and nothing it writes counts until you keep it.

Built on the real UI: the shipped phone CSS (Dock, Spell Menu, note, reel, voice recorder) and the compiled desktop Tailwind (tab bar, sync drawer, slash menu, Patronus orb). Everything is clickable. Each concept has five surfaces, A–E.

Recommended field-type set: three verbs, not eight types

Rate

A 1–N scale with an optional meaning per number. The meanings are taught on hover or tap: the number is the answer, and the anchor explains it. Covers “last day of your life (1–5)”.

Tick

A fixed list of habits to tick. Covers Sales Progress and Life Success Tracking. On the Weekly form AI ticks an item when it held on most days, and shows the count (5/6).

Write

An open answer with two settings. Shape: text · • list · 1. list · A → B pairs. Guide: optional reference text. That one verb covers open question, Freeform (no prompt), bulleted, numbered, “failed → lesson” and the guided Mr Nice Guy prompt. On the phone every Write is just talk or type. The desktop gives it its shape.

Concept 1

The Lantern Path

The evening becomes four short beats: Ki · Arrive (rate the day), Shō · Build (quick ticks), Ten · Turn (the honest questions), Ketsu · Land (what shaped you, what to reward). Each answered question lights a lantern. Patronus writes in ghost ink.

Key idea. Pacing is part of the form. The builder sorts questions into four beats, and the phone shows a brief chapter card between them, so the routine has an arc (warm-up, momentum, turn, landing) instead of being a flat list of nine questions.
Why it fits our lens. Kishōtenketsu is how Nintendo paces a level. The lanterns give quiet progress feedback without a score. Make ↔ Play lets you test the form the second you change it, the way Mario Maker does. Ghost ink keeps AI visibly separate from you until you press Keep.
Trade-off. It reorders the owner's Notion order (the ticks move earlier), and beats are one more concept to author. A form with no beats still works, but the chapter cards would feel thin on a 3-question form.
Concept 2

The Controller

One main control that changes with each question. On the phone the Dock's expanded spell turns into whatever the question needs: hold to speak, a 5-notch dial, or a tick counter. On desktop the keyboard is the controller (1–5, Space, Tab). Patronus plays as a co-op player two.

Key idea. Your thumb never goes looking for a control: it's always the bar at the bottom, and the bar becomes the right tool. On desktop, key hints show up only until you've used each verb twice, then they go away. The fastest path is also the one that teaches you.
Why it fits our lens. This is Miyamoto's “one button, many meanings”, like Mario's jump: a small set of controls that feels different in each situation. It's also how the owner already has his form: it's markdown in Notion, so the builder simply reads it.
Trade-off. With the expanded spell in the Dock there's no collapsed Back cell, so “Previous” moves into the Conditional ring. Hold-to-speak also hides the camera one tap deeper.
Concept 3

The Deck

Each question is a card. On the phone you flick a card up when you're done and down for later, and the pile grows. On desktop each answered card gets a small gold seal. Patronus writes on the back of the card, and you turn it over to read what it wrote.

Key idea. A card is something you can hold, finish and set aside, so finishing a question feels physical. “Later” is a real move (it goes to the bottom of the deck), so skipping doesn't feel like failing. AI on the back of the card means it's never mixed into your side until you pick which side to keep.
Why it fits our lens. Flick, deal, turn and stamp are Nintendo-style tactile verbs (think Clubhouse Games or a rajio-taisō stamp card). The week shows as seven stamps, not a streak number.
Trade-off. Cards hide everything but the current question on the phone, so you can't scan the whole form. Flicking is a gesture: we keep Done/Later in the ring as a fallback (see the phone-gesture lessons).
